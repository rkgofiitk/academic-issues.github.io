## Reframing AI Safety Challenges
### Human Independence, Self-Proliferation, and Coloniality

[Blog Index](../index.md)

### Introduction

Recently, a series of technology outages disrupted Microsoft, the NSA, major banks, and the US air traffic control system. The AI rollout has allowed both developers and hackers to accelerate discovery of vulnerabilities in existing systems. To prevent these security flaws from being actively exploited, organizations are deploying numerous software patches at an unprecedented speed. These mid-November system disruptions appeared to have been triggered by software failures during remedial bug-fixing. However, the subsequent replacement of the White House national cyber director by the US DoD chief information officer underscores the political and institutional significance of these technology failures. 

AI systems are  rapidly evolving into a general-purpose technology across domains, including healthcare, education, logistics and transport, public service, finance, creative arts, security and surveillance, and defense. OpenAI's announcement about AI-driven solutions for complex mathematical problems and cancer research has sparked intense debate in scientific and academic circles. However, algorithmic bias, data privacy concerns, intelligent individual surveillance and profiling, labor market disruption, and a lack of transparency are hindering the democratization of technology's benefits. This calls for effective governance mechanisms.  

In the regular column Vardi's Insight, published in the November 2026 issue of CACM, Moshe Vardi discusses various issues related to AI, drawing insights from several articles by public opinion makers in The Wall Street Journal. In this post, we examine the issues from three different perspectives regarding contemporary AI systems:
- Crossing the human dependence threshold 
- Recursive self-improvement and the prospect of self-proliferation
- The coloniality and concentration of technological power 

Together, these perspectives shift the focus of AI safety research. It is not about whether AI systems can become more intelligent than humans, but about whether these systems can eventually reduce their dependence on human support systems to approach the human independence threshold.
Human Independence and the Domestication Analogy

In the November 2026 issue of CACM, Shaon Liu's article explores how soon AI will surpass the human independence threshold. Liu draws an interesting analogy from Yuval Noah Harari's Sapiens: A Brief History of Humankind that presents the paradox of "whether wheat domesticated humans or humans domesticated wheat."  Harari argues that it was humans who reorganized their lives around cultivation, and wheat escaped its dependence on unpredictable forces of nature. As humans settled around wheat fields, they provided water and nutrient-rich care, which in turn led to wheat's growth. 

- Extending the example of wheat growth to that of AI, we find AI companies are:
- Developing new computing chips that can handle AI workloads efficiently,
- Building large data centers with secure electricity for the operation of the data centers, 
- Crowdsourcing training data from users and feeding correct prompts for responses,
- Establishing cloud platforms and payment for computation, and
- Provisioning the Internet bandwidth for human queries.
  
Consequently,  human adoption is necessary for AI's environment selection. AI does not possess a survival instinct, but it continues to expand through human adoption, investments, and maintenance. AI agents self-replicate software instances and disseminate these copies through the cloud network. However, the replica deployments depend on humans for the physical maintenance of power grids and networks, and for replacing failed equipment. 

### Recursive Self-improvement

The roots of Recursive Self-Improvement (RSI) can be traced to mathematician I. J. Good's 1965 discussion of an "intelligence explosion" in which an intelligent machine would design a more intelligent successor. ChatGPT's release notes for Codex 5.3 reveal a self-admission that its early versions were instrumental in developing the improved model, helping with training, debugging, deployment management, and failure evaluation. However,  genuine RSI and sophisticated internal automation are indistinguishable on a benchmark. Therefore, the question is whether a human is qualified to verify that AI was still in the loop when self-improvement happened. The bottleneck that resists RSI is where human tasks cannot be handed off. Most AI improvements are driven by data collection, which either requires paying people to produce and evaluate training data or setting up a pipeline to generate it. If OpenAI's admission that its earlier models contributed to the engineering feedback loop that produced its successor is accurate, then it may indicate a significant reduction in human dependence in AI improvements. Such developments could bring AI systems closer to the human independence threshold.  

### Self-proliferation and AI Safety

Often, AI safety concerns are centered on misinformation, critical decision-making errors, and economic disruption. However, a more appropriate threshold for AI safety would be when AI reaches autonomous self-proliferation capabilities. 

During the self-proliferation stage, AI agents maintain their operations by autonomously by acquiring resources and creating replicas with almost no human assistance. This capability is part of a broader ecosystem of agents, robots, factories, communication networks, and energy supply systems. Google DeepMind evaluates self-proliferation as a dangerous capability. Shaoshan Liu views AI safety as blocking AI from reaching the "human independence threshold." 

In light of these perspectives, we may redefine AI safety as the challenge of preventing AI systems from becoming capable of self-sustaining, self-reproducing, and self-expanding without significant human oversight. Currently, no AI system has reached an intelligence level necessary to maintain itself autonomously within the environments on which it depends. However, this concept offers a legitimate framework for assessing long-term technological trends and the governance challenges they present.

### Coloniality in AI Research and Development

The prospect of deploying increasingly unattended autonomous AI systems has raised questions of power imbalance, neutrality, and global justice, which are essentially manifestations of the coloniality problem. 

Historically, extractive colonialism created a significant asymmetry between imperial centers of power and the Global South. The resulting asymmetries facilitated exploitation, impoverishment of the local population, and suppression of local knowledge and development.  

In the November 2026 issue of CACM, Damian Eke elaborates on the analogous pattern of colonialism emerging from the world's largest economies' emerging AI ecosystem. Although the AI ecosystem is unlikely to reproduce a historical colonial system, it tends to reinforce existing power imbalances and economic disparities by relying on technologically superior resource-extraction mechanisms and denying other countries access to advanced technologies. 

Our insight from decolonial scholarship suggests that economic power heavily influences contemporary systems of knowledge production. The foundations for scientific research, industrial development, and technological innovation in dominant economies are established through the use of resources and wealth gained from extractive activities and colonialism. The historical accumulation of wealth and resources continues to shape the foundations of contemporary scientific research, industrial development, and capabilities of technological innovation.

The data, computational infrastructure, and expertise are confined to a small number of enterprises in technologically developed nations. These enterprises hold decision-making power, which can be viewed as a modern form of colonialism, reflecting a recurring pattern of dependency for technologically underdeveloped regions, particularly within the Global South. The manifestations of computing coloniality include:
- Data colonialism, and
- Algorithmic colonialism

### Data colonialism

Data colonialism refers to the free extraction and monetization of large amounts of data from communities. These data enable profiling and the generation of linguistic, cultural, and behavioral information that can be processed and monetized.  

For example,  Facebook offers a free basic service to users in countries across Africa, Asia, and Latin America. The service provides access to Facebook and a limited number of curated websites without charge. However, critics argue that this initiative promotes digital data colonialism. Although marketed as a free Internet service, it lacks local content and does not connect users to the global Internet. 

### Algorithmic Colonialism 

Algorithms provide a vehicle for training AI systems on data from sampled technologically advanced societies and curated, free-access initiatives provided by AI companies. However, such training data does not adequately represent the multiple linguistic, cultural, and social realities of populations and economically weaker countries. Integrating AI systems into education, employment, finance, healthcare, and public administration will perpetuate inequalities.

### Conclusion

The analogy between Harari's wheat domestication and AI development suggests that, until now, contemporary AI systems have not succeeded in breaking human dependence. However, with increasing autonomy, particularly through self-proliferation capabilities, AI systems may eventually cross the above threshold to acquire the autonomous capabilities to replicate, maintain, and provision their own resources. It underscores the need to establish governance mechanisms before such systems approach any meaningful threshold of human independence.

The benefits and burdens of AI are distributed unevenly across different geographical regions and societies. A few powerful nations and enterprises control the concentration of data and computational resources. The technological superiority of a few advanced centers will continue the domination of powerful nations. As a result, less powerful nations may find themselves subjugated under the colonial influence of a handful of AI-endowed nations. Hence, concerns about AI safety should extend beyond existential risks or the capabilities of AI models. A more fundamental issue is whether AI systems can eventually approach the human independence threshold and achieve self-sustaining growth. When this potential arises, the questions of technical safety, governance, dependency, and global power relations become intertwined.
