# UGC Allows Foreign University Campuses in India



UGC Chairperson Prof. M. Jagadish Kumar announced in a press conference that foreign universities such as Yale, Harvard, and Princeton, among others, have been invited to establish campuses in India. Before delving into the UGC's decision, I would like to analyze its implications, drawing primarily from two news reports.

- [The free press journal](https://www.freepressjournal.in/education/will-ugcs-guidelines-on-foreign-universities-in-india-fail-to-deliver), 
- [Hindu news report](https://www.thehindu.com/news/national/ugc-to-announce-guidelines-for-setting-up-foreign-universities-in-may/article66680179.ece#)

According to these reports, Prof Kumar broadly lists the following few points of the UGC's draft framework in this respect, 

- Foreign universities will retain final authority over curriculum, fees, and recruitment.  
- International higher education institutions (HEIs) must obtain approval from the UGC before establishing campuses in India.  
- Only universities ranked within the top 500 globally, either overall or in specific subjects, will be permitted to operate in the country.  
- The National Education Policy (NEP) indicated that leading global universities would be allowed in India, supported by a legislative framework.  
- This initiative aims to curb the brain drain.

According to Prof Kumar, UGC has received responses from top universities and embassies, and UGC is in the final stages of incorporating these responses. 
What are the implications of this announcement? Whatever little I can gather from the announcement is that: 

- Foreign universities will be classified as a distinct category of private universities.  
- They will not be required to seek course approvals from NAAC, AICTE, or UGC; their internal processes will suffice.  
- The Provosts, Vice-Chancellors, or Presidents of these institutions will operate independently, provided they generally adhere to the UGC's draft framework.  
- They will have the autonomy to set fees as they see fit.  
- Other central, state, or private universities will continue to operate under their current regulations.

There is no doubt that the country needs many good HEIs. However, allowing unfettered operations to foreign private universities could create 
many unforeseen problems. For example, 

- It is essential to clarify whether a consortium of foreign entities/individuals can legitimately act as a front for a foreign university to build, own, and operate a higher education institution (HEI) in India. 
- It is crucial to establish safeguards will ensure that foreign universities operate in full compliance with Indian laws while on Indian soil. 
- The methods for resolving disputes between students and employees on one side and foreign universities on the other must be clearly defined. - There should be no ambiguity regarding whether Indian courts will have jurisdiction over these disputes. 
- It is important to determine whether the regulatory framework will be instituted through executive orders or if it will require a distinct Act from the Indian Parliament.  

It is hoped that UGC has effectively addressed the aforementioned issues while managing responses from foreign embassies and universities interested in establishing campuses here.

Now, let us analyze academic decisions. Is a world ranking of 500 sufficient? In my humble opinion, the ranking of higher education institutions (HEIs) does not solely reflect their intrinsic strength. Perception plays a significant role in rankings. Many IITs and IISc enjoy better perception rankings. Notably, some new IITs do not qualify for the NIRF ranking. Does this imply that several state universities, such as JNTU, Anamalai, JU, CU, and AU, are superior to these new IITs? Why do many top-ranking JEE students opt for IITs over state universities? During my visits to a few US universities, I found that most professors I encountered regard IIT undergraduates as some of the best trained among international students. One professor even expressed her pride in having an IIT graduate in her lab and mentioned her reluctance to expose him to others for fear of poaching! Additionally, on a visit to a renowned research lab in Europe, I met several former students who were interning there. Those interns were highly sought after as research assistants. 

However, it is important to note that graduate studies at IITs differ significantly from undergraduate programs. The student intake for graduate programs at IITs is exclusively from outside the IIT system. It is widely recognized that students from outside the IIT system come with diverse backgrounds and often require preparation for graduate studies in engineering. Consequently, an average graduate student at IITs may not be on par with an average graduate student from any of the top 300 universities worldwide.

This brings us to the question: why do talented graduate students pursue admission into foreign universities? There are several reasons for this, which I will outline in order of importance.

- The GATE exam is mandatory for seeking admission into graduate studies in IITs and IISc.
- The quality of life is much better and more comfortable in the US/Europe/Canada than in India.
- The post-Ph. D. job opportunities are better in the US/Europe/Canada than in India
- Research infrastructure, even in an average North American/European/Canadian university, is better than that at even at older IITs
  
Let me explain the reasons for my order. For example, a student has to be proficient in at least four topics to qualify for GATE CSE. Consider
students from CMI, ISI, or IISERs. These students pass a Nationwide test or must have qualified in KVPY or Olympiad in their respective disciplines. However, 
they need a background in OS, Computer Organization or Architecture, and DBMS to be eligible for GATE. It puts these students in a disadvantageous position. 
Only a few such students will sit for and qualify for GATE. The requirement for GATE leaves out some good students who may have strengthened 
their graduate programs at IITs or IISc. However, there cannot be any argument about points 2-4. Furthermore, these last three points will still be 
valid even if the top 500 foreign universities set up their campuses in India. So, I cannot appreciate UGC's view about stopping brain drain.
India still has to offer the environment, space, and opportunities to employ people skilled in high-end technology to stop the brain drain.
Years back, the government of India came up with a migration program for students seeking admission into M. Tech CS. The government discontinued 
after many engineering colleges started offering B. Tech in CS. A suitably updated migration program would have benefited graduate 
programs at IITs and IISc. 

Most foreign universities in India may initially offer graduate programs in the field of Science. The emphasis on graduate studies at the Indian Institute of Science (IISc) is predominantly on this discipline. Science education at many universities and laboratories across the country is well-established. Institutions such as IISc, TIFR, Bose Institute, the Saha Institute of Nuclear Physics, PRL, NPL, IoPB, NISER, and IISER have robust Ph.D. programs in the sciences. The competitiveness of graduate studies in Science at IISc surpasses that of numerous foreign universities. Consequently, if foreign institutions place a strong emphasis on graduate studies in Science, it could impact Ph.D. admissions at the aforementioned Indian institutions. It is hoped that the UGC has taken these concerns into account in its planning.

Another area of concern is the fee structure at foreign universities, which will likely mirror those in the US, Europe, or Canada. Tuition fees for undergraduate programs typically range from USD 30,000 to 35,000. Graduate students often receive support through NSF grants or work as teaching assistants. Additionally, foreign universities operating in India will strive to compete for government-sponsored projects and funding. This competition will have dual effects, namely,

- Shrinking of fund allocation for IISc, IITs, IISER, and other CFTIs. 
- Talent migration from govt HEIs to foreign universities in India

I wonder if the government and UGC have a long-term plan to close all central institutions or relegate them to oblivion. If it comes true, then
our education system will collapse. I have seen many private players in India's higher education system. Some good institutions are coming up, but 
there are only a few. Profits drive most private players. They are more concerned only about revenue receipt than anything else. Faculty is the least
priority for these institutions. They accept students as long as the pockets of the parents are deeper. Except for a handful of institutions like BITS, 
Shivnader, Jindal, Jaypee, Munjal, Ashoka, and Bennett, all private institutions, prefer to act like teenagers' creches.  

The costs associated with foreign university campuses in India—covering faculty and support staff salaries, infrastructure, and recurring expenses—will undoubtedly be significantly lower than those in their countries of origin. However, these institutions are likely to set tuition fees at no less than USD 20,000. This approach will inevitably lead to a substantial increase in the cost of undergraduate education across India, making it increasingly unaffordable for many students. Just as in the United States, graduates may find themselves starting their careers burdened with hefty student loans.

Furthermore, this development could negatively impact premier Indian institutions such as IITs, IISc, and IISERs, as many promising faculty candidates may opt for positions at foreign universities that offer more competitive salaries. Thus, the situation presents a lose-lose scenario for higher education institutions in India. It is likely that the UGC has considered these challenges in their draft proposal permitting foreign universities to establish campuses in India.

The UGC has chosen to exert minimal control over state universities, many of which are in disarray, often becoming havens for undesirable politicians and disruptive elements. Unfortunately, some central universities are also experiencing similar challenges. This situation has adversely impacted the overall landscape of higher education in India. Consequently, young undergraduate students require assistance in achieving their aspirations for quality training in the country. There are rumors that the government intends for IITs to introduce BSc-BEd courses. While my understanding may not be entirely precise, if this is indeed the case, it appears to be a deliberate strategy to diminish the focus of IITs and limit their contribution to the advancement of higher education in India.
