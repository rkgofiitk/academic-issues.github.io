## Ph. D. of Practice Proposal: Beyond Publications & Toward Innovation 

[Blog Index](../index.md)

### Origin of the proposal

IIT Ropar has proposed a pathway in which patents, prototypes, and innovations will be considered as research output in place of a written thesis for a Ph. D. It will award a Ph. D. based on practical work, patents, and inventions of working prototypes. There has been a controversy surrounding the decision. It mainly stems from speculation that anticipated implementation-related challenges could undermine rigorous independent evaluation. 

Some observers view IIT Ropar's proposal as a reaction to the reported use of AI in thesis report preparation and a curb on predatory publications. It may not be a sufficient basis for evaluating the proposal, as IITs generally implement substantial and effective safeguards to preserve the quality of their Ph.D. programs. 

An implementation-oriented Ph.D. without a proper report is not entirely novel, though it may sound so to many at institutions across India. IIT Ropar's decision could be guided by many other factors beyond the submission of an AI-assisted thesis report, such as:

- <strong>Culture of innovation</strong>: Development of tools and inventions to support a large manufacturing ecosystem in the country.
- <strong>Research deliverables</strong>: Shifting away from paper-driven research to practical outcomes by creating high-end precision tools and products.
- <strong>Project collaboration</strong>: Recognizing that large applied capstone projects require collaboration of a team of researchers, rather than individual-centric research for a Ph.D.  
- <strong>Institute funding</strong>: Generating patents from research deliverables can earn substantial royalties for the institute and reduce its reliance on government aid.
  
Ph. D. of practice will run side by side with research-focused Ph. D. programs. Practitioners may opt for a new no-thesis Ph.D. program, while those who wish to become faculty may enroll in research-focused Ph. D. programs.

### Historical Perspectives

During British rule, Indian scientists, such as  Acharya Jagadish Chandra Bose (1858-1937), Prof. C. V. Raman (1888-1970), Homi J. Bhabha (1909-1966), and Satish Dhawan (1920-2002), earned international recognition through building instruments, devices, and equipment as proofs of concept for their theories. Side by side, self-taught industrialists G. D. Naidu (1893-1974) and Shankar Abaji Bhise(1867-1935), often referred to as the "Edisons of India," laid the groundwork for indigenous technology and mechanical innovations. 

The tradition continues at a few research organizations such as BARC, ISRO, and DRDO.

When the British left, India inherited widespread poverty, low literacy, limited industrialization, and a weak scientific infrastructure. The country's administrative, political, and educational institutions faced enormous challenges in building a modern state with universal access to education and creating the scientific and technological foundations for long-term development. 

Public investments were channeled into strategic areas to alleviate poverty through modern agricultural practices, healthcare, adult literacy, and energy security. Wars in 1962, 1965, and 1971 with China and Pakistan forced the country to divert much of its meager resources to defense and the atomic and space programs. To support high-end technological requirements, it worked to develop the IIT and NIT systems.

While the approach strengthened key national capabilities, many areas of higher education and research remain underfunded for an extended period. At present, India spends 4% of GDP, with the central government's share at 2%, compared with the ideal 6% proposed by the Kothari Commission.

### Dominance of Theoretical Research

Resource constraints and the present academic reward system have increasingly shifted the focus toward theoretical research, often at the expense of experimentation and innovation in the development of objects or devices. Although standardization in education and research has brought many benefits, including the integration of Indian academia with international research standards, it has not adequately recognized technological localization and problem-solving. 

Publications in professional journals have become a dominant, measurable indicator of research credentials and International recognition. As a result, faculty recruitment and career progression have become increasingly dependent on demonstrated academic accomplishments, such as publications in international journals and conferences, rather than on innovations in building tools or instruments.
Need for Academic Reforms

The higher education system has encountered several challenges in governance and transparency. and research integrity, such as:
Often, concerns were raised about conflicts of interest, lack of transparency, and administrative influence in academic decision-making.
Cases of plagiarism among graduate students and faculty members have often been dismissed on extraneous grounds rather than on academic grounds.

- Allegations of procedural irregularities in the accreditation processes have led to low public confidence in the regulatory bodies such as AICTE, UGC, and NAAC.
- The growth of predatory publishing, manipulated peer review, quality issues, and large-scale retractions has intensified questions and debates on research quality and integrity.
- Persistent funding disparities between centrally funded and state-funded institutions limit academic access to equal opportunities. 

Kothari Commission Report, one of the finest documents on educational reforms, including the appointments of Vice-Chancellors and Directors (chief administrators) of central universities and other important centrally funded institutes, was ignored. State universities receive substantially less funding compared to centrally funded institutions. This has led to significantly lower access to quality education among a large section of the population.

In addition to academic accountability, there are other issues with political overtones that foster conflicts among students. Two such important issues are:
- Increasing presence of mainstream political parties in student unions' activities.
- Lack of periodic rebalancing of the relative weightage assigned to caste, economic disadvantages, regional disparities, and other demographic requirements.

### Ph. D. of Practice Program

India's startup ecosystem has expanded significantly over the past decade and a half, supported by technology incubators, research parks, venture capitalists, and academia-industry collaborations. Against this background, IIT Ropar's proposal for a Ph.D. in practice program makes a strong statement of purpose.

The skepticism about the proposal mainly centered around critical issues of quality and the evaluation system rather than the foundation of the proposed program. These concerns are legitimate and deserve careful consideration. 

However, the pathway to a Ph.D. in practice acknowledges that important contributions to research and innovation may come through patents, prototypes, and products, not only through publications. It may also evolve into a strategic technology-creation framework for expanding the startup ecosystem.

### Measures For R&D

Unless the government, policymakers, administrators, and educators recognize the academic issues afflicting the country, the situation will not improve. The government should initiate the following structural reforms in educational institutions:
Adopt the recommendations of the Kothari Commission Report to reform educational institutions.
Reform reservation policy in educational institutions with equal opportunity and affirmative action for economic conditions and demographics.

Create an effective accountability mechanism for cheating, plagiarism, fraudulent research, and paper retractions.
Speed up the patent-granting process while simultaneously beginning to measure development using well-known R&D disruption metrics.
Develop strategies to reform R&D with public participation. 

The Ph. D. of Practice program is intended to reward the technological impact of research rather than publication outputs, as an alternative indicator of innovation and performance. There are well-known R&D disruption metrics:

- <strong>Consolidation Disruption Index (CDI)</strong>: Measures how new patent research papers destabilize or consolidate existing technology.
- <strong>Vitality Index</strong>: Percentage of total revenue generated by products from the new technology in a 3- to 5-year period. This indicates the extent to which the new technology is replacing existing methods.
- <strong>R&D Productivity Ratio</strong>: Ratio of cost versus the revenue generated by the new technology.
- <strong>Cycle Time to Market</strong>: Time from concept phase to commercial launch of products based on new technology.
  
Some educational institutions, such as IITs, IISc, and a few CFTIs, already have strategies to upend traditional R&D but lack the overall ecosystem to support them. IIT Madras has established a research park with technology incubation centers. Other institutions have also progressed significantly beyond their traditional bureaucratic structures, but a few inherited bottlenecks remain evident due to the lack of a transparent incentive and accountability framework. Specific overhaul measures could be:

- Shift away from secretive, closed-lab, corporate-aided collaboration to an open lab environment where crowdsourcing is possible.
- Use AI-aided discovery to accelerate innovation and shorten time to market.
- Encourage the use of rapid prototyping with continuous customer feedback loops for product improvement, rather than spending a lengthy research incubation cycle (basic research-application-development).
- Fund high-risk, high-reward research projects that will encourage serious researchers to pursue capstone research.
  
### Conclusion

Against the backdrop of the policy-versus-implementation framework debate, IIT Ropar's Ph.D. in practice is a bold step to broaden technological and scientific research beyond publications. By recognizing patents, prototypes, and technological innovations as research outcomes, the program seeks to bring doctoral education closer to societal needs. Its success will depend on removing implementation bottlenecks, maintaining rigorous evaluation standards, and ensuring independent quality assurance. If implemented effectively,  it could be a valuable complement to traditional research-oriented doctoral programs while contributing to India's developing startup ecosystem.
