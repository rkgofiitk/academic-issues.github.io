## AI will replace 40% of Jobs: Myth versus Reality.

[Blog Index](../index.md)

Recently, Sam Altman repeated his claim that AI will replace 40% of the jobs. Let us dissect the reality versus myth created by Altman's utterances. Sam clarified later that his exact statement is "I can easily imagine a world where 30 to 40 per cent of the tasks that happen in the economy today get done by AI in the not very distant future."  Consider the end-to-end intellectual input plus the time from beginning to finish a job. Does it mean that AI can contribute 0.4x of the requirement? It is unlikely. However, Sam defends his utterance with a clever interplay of words to indicate:
- Suppose a job is composed of 10 finite tasks.
- Then the AI assistant can perform 4.
- So,  6 out of 10 remain incomplete for the job.
  
The explanation deliberately leaves room for misrepresentation. Consider the following question:
 - Is there a scope for a task to remain incomplete?
   
As we understand from Sam Altman's statement, the intuitive meaning is that tasks are such that an AI assistant can perform each to completion or leave it entirely.   There is no scope for any task being partially completed or half done. So, are we missing something? Indeed, the critical point we are missing in Altman's statement is the granularity of task division for a job. He meant only an atomic task, not just any task. 
So, the answer to the above question is NO because otherwise, the job decomposition will be incorrect. No atomic tasks can remain incomplete by definition, no matter who performs. So, Sam Altman's statement is eventually correct if we add the qualifier atomic to define a task in the job decomposition. 

While Sam Altman's comment is fundamentally correct, some may still have questions:
- Did OpenAI create a benchmark suite for testing the capabilities of AI assistants?
- Was enough research done to establish an ideal job mix?
- Did OpenAI conduct established statistical estimations to claim the hypothesis that an AI assistant can complete 40% of atomic tasks?
  
I hope they have adequate answers to all three questions above; otherwise, Sam is only trying to create a sales pitch.

Let us assume that Sam Altman is technically correct about the capabilities of AI assistants, and analyse the confusion it created. The typical definition of a task and a job, as I can find, is:
- Task: "A task is a piece of work to be done or attempted."
- Job: "An individual piece of work or task."
  
Technically speaking,  there is no separation between a job and a task. So, there is no way an AI assistant can complete 40% of a job. However, when the OpenAI CEO says something, the market reacts, and many take it seriously. It creates needless panic, especially among fresh job seekers. If we carefully understand the game, the same view is expressed by CEOs of Google, Microsoft, Nvidia, and Anthropic. Every one of these companies has invested heavily in AI and wants to bet big on the success of AI assistants in next-generation automation. It will reduce the need for human experts and recurring annual company costs. The net result is an increase in profitability. We will come to this analysis thread a little later. 

However, let us try to seek an answer to the fundamental question concerning the capabilities of AI assistants:
Can an AI assistant partition a job into atomic tasks?
Let us consider two principal types of jobs depending on the domain of applications. In domains with high stakes, such as medicine, law, or governance, it is essential to realize that AI assistants cannot lead in decomposing a job. Any decomposition decision left to AI assistants may lead to results that stakeholders can contest vehemently. Medicine is primarily empirical, based on human experience, though surgery is reasonably precise. Even in surgery, contingent cases may arise with human life at stake. Similarly, in law or in governance, the stakes are understandably high. However, there are high-volume jobs with low stakes, such as data entry, logistics, and customer service. We can let AI assistants perform task decompositions, but with human oversight. Then there are hybrid job types. So, the net outcome of achieving task-level job decomposition using AI assistants is extremely low. Only human experts can perform job decomposition and identify the atomic tasks that an AI assistant can perform. 

For example, assume a job like accounting. We can decompose it into smaller tasks such as:
- Data entry
- Reconciliation
- Forecasting
- Reporting
  
What can we accomplish through an AI assistant? It can achieve specific repetitive but atomic tasks such as data entry, interest calculation on bank borrowing rates, revenue receipts on sales, etc. Altman's comment on AI assistants wiping out 40% of jobs means they work as atomic task optimizers rather than the conventional meaning of the task. So, AI assistants will raise the productivity of a human expert. Either way, the requirement for human experts will gradually reduce as we adapt to AI assistants. However, it has a flip side, as a few companies have started firing fresh programmers. As senior engineers eventually ease out and retire, there will be a vacuum in trained human talent. The void will be difficult to fill. 

Now, let us return to the other thread of discussion, namely, increased profitability by deployment of AI assistants. The companies are doing well, but why is a profit increase necessary? I asked Copilot to spill the annual earnings of some of these companies. The result is an interesting revelation. Google made $34.54 billion in just two quarters of 2025. Nvidia is in the same bracket, Microsoft came second with $27.23 billion, and Meta made a profit of over $20 billion. However, OpenAI 's loss now stands at $13 billion. Although Anthropic's revenue tripled, it is operating at a substantial loss. Google, Microsoft, and Meta have made huge investments in AI.  Nvidia's H100 and Blackwell chips are gold standards for AI workloads. So, the pressure on all these companies, specifically AI companies, is mounting from stakeholders and investors. 

In the context of profits, let me mention Sridhar Vembu's candid remark on the pressure to open Zoho to public investment in response to a sudden surge in registrations for **Arattai** by 100x in just three days. Vembu says he still wants to retain it as a privately held company. He prefers the company to focus on research and innovation in preference to the pressure of profitability.
