## Moonlighting: The Story of Soham Parekh

[Blog Index](../index.md)

An interesting case concerning the moonlighting of a software engineer named Soham Parekh is all over the Internet. Mr. Suhail Doshi, CEO of an image generation startup, posted a [cautionary note about Soham's moonlighting to alert]( https://x.com/Suhail/status/1940294149955358882) potential employers. According to Mr. Doshi, Soham was simultaneously working as a remote tech employee for at least 3-4 US-based startups. He accused Soham of targeting [YC startups](../YC_founders_IITs.md) for remote work as a moonlighter. A few other startup CEOs also alleged that Soham was on their payroll during an overlapping period. It was unclear whether they suspected Soham of time-slicing working hours for moonlighting. Mr. Doshi's post was also silent on two issues, as were the other CEOs who may have fired Soham in the past:
- Did anyone discover that Soham was working for several startups simultaneously?
- Did Soham lack competence and fail to deliver the assigned tasks?

Mr. Doshi's post also did not complain about the lack of competence, though he doubted Soham's educational background. Soham's LinkedIn profile says that he is with Dynamo AI. Ironically, the company specializes in AI delivering auditable AI guardrails, hallucination checks, red-beaming, and observability. The company could perhaps develop auditable AI guardrails against moonlighting employees.


Soham came forward to tell his side of the story. He worked for 20 hours a day and seven days a week. None of the startup CEOs of the companies that offered jobs to Soham in the past had any issues with his technical competence. Many of them have interviewed him rigorously and have given him offers. It is perplexing to learn that no interviewer could suspect that the candidate has hopped from many companies in a short duration. The Indian government and the public sector have a practice of background checks through state security agencies. Unless the concerned district SP does the attestation, the candidate remains a temporary employee and can be dismissed from the service without notice. Of course, startups do not have any elaborate process for carrying out an elaborate attestion process before employing an engineer. However, they have HR units responsible for some sort of a vetting process. From Mr. Doshi's post, it appears that his company did not even have an HR unit to conduct a background check through an outsourced agency. 

I learned from a reliable source that Soham is a competent developer with time management issues. He evaded meetings and often requested reschedulings. My source also tells me they realized Soham could not deliver according to the promised schedule. That is why he was fired from the company after 4-5 months. One thing that struck me is that no one could discover that Soham held concurrent responsibilities for several companies, even when exploring his background of frequent job hopping. I checked many LinkedIn posts that say Soham might have worked for over 30 companies at a time, which is astonishing. His social network footprint must be very revealing. However, Mr. Doshi's post raised a few points regarding practice and work ethics:
- Is it ethically wrong to be a moonlighter if a person can preserve NDAs and contract obligations?
- Does the employer buy an exclusive right to the employee's time, especially in a remote job contract?
- Does the employment contract say an employee cannot work simultaneously for two or more jobs?

The point of raising the above issues comes from my earlier post about large-scale [moonlighting during Covid-19 lockdown](./MoonShining.md). Many companies like Wipro, TCS, HCL, Infosys enforced pre-COVID contracts with their employees and asked them to return to offices after the COVID-19 scare receded. Startups have a different work culture. Many expect founding engineers to work exclusively for the company without caring much about the time management issues and encrochment into personal space, but expect competence and exclusive time commitments. I know a lot of such instances when a startup expands too much before turning into a public company and announcing IPOs. Many of such US-based companies operate in India and often exploit field workers. One specific US-based company I know of works in fintech, operating PoS devices and software. A field employee typically gets around 30-50k Indian Rupees (equivalent to 4-5k USD). The employee is expected to visit the merchant establishment and troubleshoot PoS devices. The company monitors merchant calls by each employee. The performance assessments are done according to merchant calls and employees' abilities to resolve issues. These are gruelling tasks, even involving the resolution of technical low-end problems. The field employees were exhausted and drained out entirely due to hot and humid weather, traveling in buses and trains without AC to attend merchant calls. Furthermore, we have the likes of Narayan Murthy of Infosys and [S. N. Subramanian, CEO of Larsen and Toubro](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t12s66T0v4g), who want  commitments ranging from 70 to 90 hours a week from their employees. Unethical exploitations like this are frequent across all established companies and startups. ESOP is used as a carrot to retain employees. Yet many tech companies experience high attrition rates exceeding 10-15%. 

On the other hand, some startups have established a very sound policy of employee and employer relationships. I raised the moonlighting issue with one of my students who established a cloud service startup in Bangalore a year and a half back. Starting with four founding engineers, his company now employs over 100 engineers. He is clear in his approach and says that moonlighting by an employee hardly matters. It is acceptable if the employee can deliver according to the project deadlines and respects NDA compliances. He says trust plays a significant role in the business. Of course, we did not discuss the issue of scalability. Big and established tech companies like Wipro or Infosys, or any company employing over 3k or 5k employees, cannot work on trust alone. These companies view the employer-employee relationship in a traditional framework that relies on full-time employees who can commit more than 40-45 hours a week. 

The mass media don't find much of an issue with sophisticated ways of exploiting workers by intruding into their personal spaces after office hours. They mention exploitation as a casual statement without going into details, like what is happening on the ground, as in the case of a US-based fintech company mentioned above. Soham's case has gone viral on mass and social media. However, according to the [Wall Street Journal)](https://www.wsj.com/lifestyle/careers/how-a-side-hustle-can-boost-your-job-performance-11635533208?gaa_at=eafs&gaa_n=ASWzDAiaaKmi9Wdsakwx7c4bkGNvBYJE-BW3fxcXb2M1j1QaWY2FyY6kG-62_9LxN3s%3D&gaa_ts=68790b76&gaa_sig=n7bZVdsjA9WOupoFig9oSLZA6k1j2KS7ldAzb5FLATe_vNW5NFHvOYluedyzYg_rGLEYnMC3zoifNZ0NiE477w%3D%3D), about 5.3% of the skilled segment moonlighted. Remote employees, in all practical purposes, are freelance consultants. Employers often resort to unethical ways and means to keep employees engaged beyond the time they are paid for. For example, scheduling remote meetings and discussions beyond designated office hours and holidays. So, while individual greed is a significant motivation for Soham Parekh's aberrations, we need to examine its ramifications beyond moonlighting.
- Whether the employer offers a decent workplace
- Whether the employer offers a decent package to its employees
- Whether the employee fulfills norms and obligations for the assigned job
- Whether the employment contract complies with state labor laws

Operating in compliance with all regulations may be unfeasible for startups engaged in research and innovations. So, the trust between the employer and employees is critical to success. However, the noise around Soham Parekh's moolinghting is a case of "much ado about nothing." He prefers to work like a freelancer, so let him be. He expressed exactly this sentiment in a recent X-post. Naming and shaming an engineer for moolinghting harms startups rather than individuals.
