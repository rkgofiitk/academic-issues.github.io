## Training AI models with Print Books and Digital Content

[Back to Index](../index.md)

Anthropic [bought print books worth millions USD](https://simonwillison.net/2025/Jun/24/anthropic-training/) in February 2024. The company employed service providers to disassemble books and cut their contents into pages for digital scanning, after which the physical books were discarded. The organization used the extracted content to train AI models, leveraging knowledge from these works. It raised the question of whether Anthropic violated copyright laws. Three authors—Andrea Bartz, Charles Graeber, and Kirk Wallace Johnson—filed a lawsuit against the company, alleging that it unlawfully appropriated knowledge from their books to train its AI models and profited from that practice. However, a federal judge ruled in favor of Anthropic, asserting that using legally purchased books for training without the authors' consent constitutes fair use. Human readers utilize their cognitive abilities to internalize knowledge from books before applying it in relevant contexts. The court used the above rationale to reach the decision. We can draw the following important conclusion from the court's ruling: 
- AI models possess the cognitive infrastructure and abilities to internalize knowledge from books and apply it like humans.

The Antrhopic case reminds us of a late 1990 case of [A & M Record, Inc. vs Napster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A%26M_Records,_Inc._v._Napster,_Inc.). Napster's peer-to-peer file sharing allowed users to share copyrighted audio files online. A&M Records held Napster liable for contributory vicarious copyright violations as it did not take permission for facilitating users to share copyrighted audio files without authorization. The US Court of Appeals for the 9th Circuit affirmed the trial court's verdict that Napster could be held liable for contributory and vicarious infringements of Intellectual Property rights regarding audio file sharing. The similarity between the Anthropic and Napster cases is striking in that  AI models would ultimately respond to users' queries using acquired knowledge from books. Though there is no explicit peer-to-peer sharing, the solutions from AI models may spread far and wide among a large user base, often crossing geographical boundaries. Considering the disparate views of the US courts between the two cases involving IPR, it appears that IPR issues have evolved over the last 25 years in view of the courts.  

However,  it is unclear if the courts have examined the consequential application of responses from AI models. One of the crucial questions raised by the Anthropic case is whether the company that created AI models may be held contributory and vicariously liable as an accomplice in criminal activities. It may be particularly relevant if AI-assisted models are misused to facilitate criminal activities. The judge's ruling in the Anthropic case did not address the implications of outputs generated by AI models based on the knowledge acquired from various texts. These outputs, which comprise responses to prompts, raise significant ethical questions. AI models will use information from digitally acquired knowledge from texts to generate responses; such outputs fall within the realm of AI model output. If we consider AI models analogous to human entities during their training on texts, there seems to be little rationale for the judge to overlook the legality of these outputs. 

Unlike human entities, AI models are not subject to bandwidth constraints and can handle potentially millions of prompts quickly. It will be interesting to see whether the judge implements a maximum threshold for AI model usage. However, enforcing a limit may introduce different challenges related to auditing and enforcing a reasonable threshold. It highlights the complexity of regulating AI and the potential difficulties in applying traditional legal frameworks to this rapidly evolving technology.

The other question that the federal judge has not decided yet is the accusation that Anthropic has pirated millions of books from the Internet. Pirating books is a more straightforward issue than training. However, if books are already available on the Internet and Anthropic has complied with the declared licensing agreements, then the pirating issue will not stand. It is already a significant victory for Anthropic, notwithstanding the two other pending matters before the court. I am sure many other AI companies, like OpenAI, Google, etc., must also be closely monitoring the outcome of verdicts from suing Anthropic. 

In the Indian context, copyright law offers substantial automatic protection without registration. It comprehensively covers various creative works, including multimedia and digital content. The copyright law provides both criminal and civil remedies in the cases of infringements. However, whether the training of programming models will be seen as an infringement by Indian courts remains untested. However, our experience tells us that the Indian judicial system is notoriously inadequate and slow in enforcing provisions related to various Acts. It may raise concerns among the creative content generators.
